Special K Red Berries: A Hit Piece

We have been patient. We have said nothing. We have stood in the cereal aisle, read the box, poured the bowl, eaten around the berries, and said nothing, because we believed — naively, generously, in defiance of all available evidence — that Special K Red Berries deserved the benefit of the doubt.

The benefit of the doubt has been revoked.

I. Red Berries. Which Ones. And Why.

The box says Red Berries. Not strawberries. Not "with strawberries." Red Berries — as if Kellogg's convened a focus group, landed on the most aggressively vague descriptor available, and printed it on the box with the confidence of a company that has never once been asked a follow-up question. IT'S JUST STRAWBERRIES.

We are asking a follow-up question. Not about which berries. About why there are berries at all.

Nobody asked for this. The flakes were fine. The flakes were doing their job. They were a neutral, inoffensive grain delivery system with a reasonable nutritional profile and no particular personality, which is exactly what a weekday morning requires. The flakes had a future. And then somebody, in a conference room somewhere, decided that what the flakes needed was fruit, and the fruit they chose was strawberries, and the strawberries they sourced were freeze-dried, and the name they gave to all of this was Red Berries, and now we are here.

We did not want the berries. We have never wanted the berries. The berries are not why we are buying this cereal.
II. The Strawberries Specifically

Let us discuss the freeze-dried strawberries, because they are the entire premise and they have not earned that status.

A freeze-dried strawberry is a strawberry that has been convinced to give up everything that made it a strawberry. The moisture is gone. The texture is gone. The sense of being a real object in the world is gone. What remains is a strawberry-shaped memory — a structural echo of fruit, light as paper, dissolving on contact into a vaguely sweet powder that turns the milk a color that can only be described as concerning.

These are what the box is named after. The entire product identity rests on an ingredient that tastes like a strawberry that has been through something and would rather not discuss it.

And they are everywhere. They are distributed throughout every layer of the box with a generosity that is completely inverted from what was wanted. Some bowls have eight. Some bowls have twelve. The flakes, which are the point, which are what we came for, are simply the medium through which the freeze-dried strawberries travel from the box to the bowl we did not want them in.

III. The Cereal Itself

The flakes. The actual cereal component. The thing this should have been about, and yet.

They are fine. They are technically food. They have a flavor that exists and a texture that begins somewhere in the vicinity of acceptable before making its inevitable journey toward a soggy conclusion nobody asked for. There is nothing wrong with the flakes in any specific, articulable way. There is also nothing right about them. They taste like the color beige. They taste like a cereal that was designed in a meeting where nobody was having a good time.

Special K Red Berries is not a bad cereal. It is worse than a bad cereal. It is a mid cereal that named itself after the one part of it that is actively unwanted, which is a level of misplaced confidence this publication has rarely encountered outside of a performance review.

The flakes did not save this. The flakes were never going to save this. The flakes needed saving themselves and instead got saddled with freeze-dried strawberries, which is the cereal equivalent of two people in a failing relationship deciding to get a dog.

IV. What Happens at the End of the Bowl

Here is where we must be honest about what this cereal produces.

The flakes are eaten. The milk is drunk. And what remains, floating (because freeze dried strawberries refuse to sink), is the problem — a collection of freeze-dried strawberries.

They cannot be consumed. Not because they are inedible, technically, but because the decision has already been made. They were not wanted at the beginning of the bowl. They are not wanted at the end of the bowl. They have always been here under false pretenses and everyone at the table knows it.

And so they are spooned, carefully, into the waiting mouths of the dogs.

The dogs eat them. The dogs eat everything. The dogs have no position on freeze-dried strawberries. The dogs are not the point. They eat rabbit poop with delight.

The point is that this cereal has produced a second breakfast course — an unwanted berry course, served on a spoon, to animals — and this is apparently the intended endpoint. This is what Kellogg's has engineered. A product whose defining feature becomes, in practice, supplemental dog food.
V. Conclusion

Special K Red Berries is a mid cereal held hostage by its own name. The flakes were never going to carry it. The berries were never going to help. And yet here we are, every morning, eating around something we didn't want, spooning the evidence to the dogs, waiting for Kellogg's to figure out what they have done.

They will not figure out what they have done.

The dogs will be fed.
